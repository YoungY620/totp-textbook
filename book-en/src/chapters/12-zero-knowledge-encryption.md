<div class="hook">"Not even our own engineers can see your passwords," the pitch goes, "and you would stay safe even if our servers were breached." Take that literally — as any customer is entitled to — and it becomes a precise cryptographic claim: a server that lies to your client, reorders your data, weakens your key derivation and rewrites your sharing keys still cannot touch your vault. That is the exact standard three popular cloud managers are held to here, and the results are not marketing.</div>

## 1. Background and the motivating threat

A cloud password manager encrypts a user's passwords into a single **vault**, under a key derived from the master password, and uploads the ciphertext to a server that never sees the plaintext — the shape of end-to-end encrypted (E2EE) storage. Vendors market this property as **Zero Knowledge Encryption**, a term the authors note has no strict technical meaning (it is not zero-knowledge proofs) yet conveys a strong promise: that the server learns nothing about the vault.

The three vendors studied — **Bitwarden, LastPass and Dashlane**, together more than **60 million users and 23% of the market** — make claims that, read plainly, promise protection even if the server is **fully malicious**: Bitwarden says its team "cannot see your passwords"; Dashlane says attackers "can't steal the information, even if Dashlane's servers are compromised"; LastPass says no one but the user has the master password. None states an attacker model, yet a customer could reasonably infer that a compromised server is survivable. That inference defines the paper's **threat model**: a server that can arbitrarily deviate from its expected behaviour. Three arguments justify it: the vendors' own claims imply it; concentrated, high-value secrets make these servers attractive targets for advanced attackers (LastPass was breached in 2015 and 2022, the latter reaching an encrypted corporate vault and backup keys); and in E2EE messaging and cloud storage, security against a malicious server is already the accepted reading of "end-to-end encrypted". The trivial *malicious-client* attack (a backdoored client) is set aside, since signed releases make it detectable and risky for a vendor.

From this the authors derive the security expectations a user should have: **confidentiality** of all vault data (credentials, card data, notes); **integrity** of vault data and metadata, at the level of individual items *and* of the vault as a whole; and **secure sharing and account recovery** for the intended parties only. Achieving them needs subsidiary properties, e.g. resistance to a server that weakens client cryptography by lowering the KDF iteration count. Because a malicious server may still need the honest client to react, **client interaction** is treated as a cost: Table 1 records whether each attack needs a routine action (`sync`, `login`, `open` the vault) or a rarer one (`join` an organisation, `share`, or a misleading `dialog`). Most attacks need only routine actions.

## 2. The three managers and their key hierarchies

The analysis follows the "Cryptography in the Wild: attack" methodology — read white papers and client code, model each feature as pseudo-code, attack the model, then confirm with a proof-of-concept malicious server against a real client. Bitwarden was studied first (source available); Dashlane and LastPass were partly reverse-engineered. Without formal analysis, the authors call their attack set **incomplete** — in places they stopped once they had enough severe findings.

**Bitwarden.** Master password and email seed a KDF (PBKDF2 or Argon2id) that outputs a 32-byte master key $k_{m}$; authentication sends $h_{m}$, one PBKDF2 iteration over $k_{m}$ with the password as salt, checked against the value stored at registration. HKDF-Expand with labels `enc` and `mac` derives a 64-byte extended master key. A random 64-byte **user key** $k_{u} = k_{u}^{enc} \,\|\, k_{u}^{mac}$ protects all vault data and is stored on the server encrypted under the extended master key. A 2048-bit RSA keypair serves organisations, its private key encrypted under $k_{u}$. Encryption is AES-CBC with HMAC (the HMAC is omitted for backwards compatibility when the key is only 32 bytes). Since client version 2024.2.0, new items use individually sampled **per-item keys**; older items remain directly under $k_{u}$. Organisations share a 64-byte symmetric key $k_{org}$ encrypted under each member's public key, plus an organisation RSA keypair for recovery.

**LastPass.** The password runs through PBKDF2 (email as salt) into a 32-byte user key $k_{u}$, with a default of **600,000** iterations; authentication sends $h_{u}$, one further PBKDF2 iteration over $k_{u}$ with the password as salt. Vault items use AES-CBC with **no integrity protection**. A user RSA keypair has its secret key CBC-encrypted under $k_{u}$; sharing uses shared folders with a 32-byte $k_{shr}$ encrypted RSA-OAEP to recipients' public keys.

**Dashlane.** The vault is a **transactional database**: each transaction carries unencrypted type and timestamp metadata plus encrypted content. A leading **payload string** names the KDF (PBKDF2, Argon2d, or no derivation), its parameters, the cipher (AES-256) and the mode (default CBC with HMAC). A 32-byte intermediate key $k_{cbc}$ is derived from the password; CBC-with-HMAC adds a SHA-512 step for a 64-byte $k_{cbc\text{-}hmac}$. A 2048-bit RSA keypair supports sharing via a fresh $k_{shr}$ encrypted to recipients. The description is partly reverse-engineered, since the only fully open-source client is a CLI that lacks encryption and sharing.

## 3. The attacks: four categories, 25 attacks

Proof-of-concept malicious servers yield **25 attacks**: **12 against Bitwarden (BW), 7 against LastPass (LP), 6 against Dashlane (DL)**, grouped in Table 1 by cause into **key escrow**, **item-level vault encryption**, **sharing**, and **backwards compatibility**. Table 1 marks attacks whose impact includes **recovery of encrypted passwords** with a dagger; **12 of the 25** carry it, and the paper states that "the majority of the attacks allow recovery of passwords". Impacts range from metadata tampering to the **complete compromise of every vault in an organisation**.

**Key escrow.** **BW01 (Malicious Auto-Enrolment)** — when a user joins *any* organisation the client fetches organisation data with no integrity protection; the server can enable auto-enrolment and substitute its own organisation public key, so the client encrypts the user key to the adversary. Impact: full vault compromise (dagger), needing only that the user join an organisation — the invitation may be perfectly honest. Because an organisation's private key is known to all its members, the attack **spreads worm-like** across overlapping recovery-enabled organisations. **BW02 (Malicious Key Rotation)** repeats the trick at key rotation, when $pk_{org}$ is re-fetched over the same unauthenticated sync. **LP01 (Malicious Password Reset)** — super-admin public keys are fetched at each login and not authenticated, and LastPass clients upload the recovery ciphertexts even for users not enrolled, so a single login leaks the user key. All three are full-vault compromises (dagger).

**Item-level encryption.** Each field is encrypted separately with no binding to its item or field name, so integrity holds only per field. **BW05 / LP02 (Item Field Swapping)** let the server swap ciphertexts within or across items, or merge fields into new items. Chained with **BW06 / LP03 (Icon URL Item Decryption)** it leaks secrets: both clients decrypt a URL field and fetch the icon from a server that receives the URL's domain, so moving a username or password ciphertext into the URL field reads it back (Bitwarden imposes plaintext conditions; LastPass does not). Bitwarden's July 2024 URL checksum protects only newer items with per-item keys — which **BW10** can suppress. **BW07 / LP04 / DL04 (Remove KDF Iterations)** exploit unauthenticated, unencrypted KDF settings. Bitwarden's derivation costs $N+1$ iterations, so moving from $N=600{,}000$ to $N=1$ drops 600,001 to 2 — a **~300,000x speed-up** on the master password. LastPass can be pushed to 3 iterations (inner minimum 2, outer fixed 1), a **200,000x speed-up**; Dashlane's forged settings transaction drops PBKDF2 from 200,000 iterations to 1, again **200,000x**. Also here: **BW04 / LP06** (unprotected item metadata), **LP05** (malleable vault), and **DL01 (Transaction Replay)** — the transaction key is not unique, so the server can silently duplicate, reorder or drop transactions.

**Sharing.** All three share items and none authenticate public keys. **BW08 (Organisation Injection)** adds a user to arbitrary organisations and chains into **BW03/BW09**; **BW09 (Organisation Overwrite)** swaps in an adversary-controlled organisation key on sync, undetectably (organisation compromise, dagger). **LP07 / DL02 (Sharing Key Overwrite)** substitute the recipient's RSA public key during a share, so the shared-folder key is encrypted to the adversary (shared-vault compromise, dagger).

**Backwards compatibility.** The server triggers legacy code paths. In Bitwarden the encryption-type byte is not integrity-protected, so a known-plaintext gadget (**BWb1**) plus bit-flipping gives **BW11 (User Key Overwriting)**, recovering $k_{u}^{enc}$ and voiding the confidentiality of later items (dagger), and **BW12 (Downgrade to Legacy)**, which strips the MAC key for CBC-only mode — full compromise once combined with a padding oracle (dagger). Both need two login attempts. In Dashlane, CBC-only mode is still accepted, enabling padding oracles (**DLb1/DLb2**) and the chained **DL03 (Item Injection)**, **DL04**, **DL05 (CBC-Only Downgrade)** (dagger) and **DL06 (Lucky 64)**, which need on the order of $10^{4}$–$10^{5}$ syncs — over 126 days at normal attack speed, but "reasonable for a targeted attack".

The recurring root causes are anti-patterns: missing public-key authentication, unauthenticated organisation metadata, no whole-vault integrity, insufficient key separation, and legacy CBC that the server can remotely re-enable.

## 4. Mitigations, scope and what it means in practice

The paper maps every attack to a combination of five remedies: **authenticated encryption (AE)**, **key separation (KS)**, **authenticated data for unavoidably-plaintext fields (AD)**, **public-key authentication (PKA)** and **ciphertext authentication (SC)**. Concretely: drop legacy CBC; extend Bitwarden's per-item keys to per-field and make them mandatory; authenticate metadata and security-critical settings such as KDF parameters; and authenticate or *sign* public-key ciphertexts, for which the authors favour **signcryption**, because public-key encryption alone cannot provide origin authentication (anyone can encrypt). Public keys ultimately need a **PKI** (whose CA is not the server operator), Trust-On-First-Use, or a messaging-style Verified Key Directory; key escrow can be hardened with **HSMs**, as Signal/WhatsApp and Apple's Advanced Data Protection do. The fixes require **breaking changes**, so the authors propose a dedicated migration-only client to avoid bricking users' vaults.

**When does the malicious-server model apply?** The authors hold that it is justified: the guarantees are advertised, the data is concentrated and high-value, and adjacent E2EE domains already assume it — and covert server tampering is hard to detect, especially when targeted. On whether ordinary users should abandon cloud managers, their position is **no**: the stringent conditions mean **most users would not be targeted**, while higher-risk individuals and organisations "could be at substantial risk". The paper also concedes the attacks may already be known to advanced threat actors.

**Limits and transfer.** The attack set is explicitly **incomplete** (no formal analysis), and PoC testing covered Bitwarden's web client and the LastPass/Dashlane extensions, though most attacks are design-level and client-independent. The lessons transfer well beyond the three vendors: "PKI cannot be avoided", "public-key encryption is not authenticated", "item-level encryption is not vault integrity", and "backwards compatibility is a liability" all recur in E2EE cloud storage and messaging, and the authors judge password managers now match modern E2EE cloud storage in complexity. Rollback remains open: Merkle trees can authenticate vault structure but cannot prevent full rollback without a trusted third party or local state, which conflicts with the "master password on any stateless device" expectation. Overall the paper is **reformist rather than abolitionist**: keep cloud managers, fix the cryptography, and pin down what "zero knowledge" may mean.

## 5. Related literature and the state of the field

- **Z. Li, W. He, D. Akhawe, D. Song, *The Emperor's New Password Manager: Security Analysis of Web-based Password Managers*, USENIX Security 2014** ([usenix.org](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity14/technical-sessions/presentation/li)) — the founding critique of web password managers (chapter 8); its threat model is weaker, and none of the early work considered a malicious server.
- **S. Oesch, S. Ruoti, *That Was Then, This Is Now: A Security Evaluation of Password Generation, Storage, and Autofill in Browser-based Password Managers*, USENIX Security 2020** ([usenix.org](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity20/presentation/oesch)) — the 2020 re-evaluation concluding managers had improved (chapter 9); this paper is the rejoinder, since improvement in the honest-server model had not delivered malicious-server security.
- **A. Fábrega, A. Namavari, R. Agarwal, B. Nassi, T. Ristenpart, *Exploiting Leakage in Password Managers via Injection Attacks*, USENIX Security 2024** ([usenix.org](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity24/presentation/fabrega)) — injection attacks under a malicious-*client* plus passive-server model (chapter 10); complementary, since that work assumes a mostly honest server.
- **Y. Duan, D. Wang, Y. Fu, *Security Analysis of Master-Password-Protected Password Management Protocols*, IEEE S&P 2025** ([IEEE](https://www.computer.org/csdl/proceedings-article/sp/2025/471500a701/26JLYLtitl6)) — a formal analysis of master-password protocols (chapter 11), cited as neighbouring work and the kind of treatment the authors want for the malicious-server setting.
- **G. Avoine, X. Carpent, D. Leblanc-Albarel, *In the Vault, but Not Safe: Exploring the Threat of Covert Password Manager Providers*, ePrint 2025/1278** ([eprint.iacr.org](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/1278)) — the "parallel preprint" the authors contrast with their own: it assumes a *covert* rather than fully malicious server, classifies managers into three types and sketches attacks without PoCs or full impact analysis.
- **E2EE cloud storage as the nearest neighbour.** Albrecht et al., *Share with Care: Breaking E2EE in Nextcloud*, IEEE EuroS&P 2024 ([eprint 2024/546](https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/546)); Backendal et al., *MEGA: Malleable Encryption Goes Awry*, IEEE S&P 2023 ([eprint 2022/959](https://eprint.iacr.org/2022/959)); and Backendal et al., *A Formal Treatment of End-to-End Encrypted Cloud Storage*, CRYPTO 2024 ([eprint 2024/989](https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/989)) — supplying the malicious-server norm, the public-key-authentication failures, and the formal definitions the authors want password managers to borrow.

The authors' overall judgement is that the field has **no gold standard and no precise security notions** to aim at, unlike TLS or Signal, and that its legacy — LastPass dates to 2008 — left residues of early-2000s cryptography that backwards compatibility preserves. They position their work as the first to establish the need for formal malicious-server definitions for password managers, paralleling what is only now emerging for E2EE cloud storage.

## 6. Summary and Callbacks

- The target is the **"Zero Knowledge Encryption"** marketing claim, read as security against a **fully malicious server**; three vendors — **Bitwarden, LastPass, Dashlane**, over **60 million users and 23% of the market** — are analysed under that model.
- The paper presents **25 attacks** — **12 BW, 7 LP, 6 DL** — in four categories: key escrow, item-level vault encryption, sharing, and backwards compatibility. Table 1 marks **12** with a dagger (recovery of encrypted passwords); the paper states the majority allow password recovery.
- **Key escrow** attacks (BW01, BW02, LP01) recover the full vault from a routine action such as joining an organisation or logging in, because organisation/admin public keys are unauthenticated; BW01 spreads worm-like.
- **Item-level** design breaks whole-vault integrity (BW05/LP02 swapping), enabling secret leakage via icon-URL fetches (BW06/LP03) and KDF-iteration downgrades of **~300,000x** (Bitwarden) and **200,000x** (LastPass, Dashlane).
- **Sharing** fails on unauthenticated public keys (BW09, LP07, DL02); **backwards compatibility** lets the server force legacy CBC and trigger key-overwriting and downgrade attacks (BW11, BW12, DL03–DL06).
- The root causes are anti-patterns — missing public-key authentication, no whole-vault integrity, insufficient key separation, remote-triggerable legacy code — curable with AE, key separation, authenticated data, public-key authentication and signcryption.
- In practice the authors do **not** tell ordinary users to abandon cloud managers: the attacks need stringent (mostly malicious-server) conditions, so most users are unlikely to be targeted, while high-risk individuals and organisations could be at substantial risk. The right response is vendor patching plus formal security definitions.
- The broader lesson — echoing the book's thesis — is that "cloud + zero-knowledge claim" repeats the shared-secret pattern: security must be argued against a malicious server, not assumed from marketing, and the durable fix is authenticated, well-separated keys (and, for phishing, moving to public-key authenticators in chapter 13).

**Callback.** This closes the fifth act. Chapter 1 opened the book with Lamport taking the secret off the server so that a stolen password file would be worthless; chapters 3 and 4 put a shared key back on the server for convenience; chapters 8 through 12 have followed that single decision through the password-manager world. The gap this chapter names — between a "zero knowledge" brochure and a cryptographically demonstrable guarantee — is where chapter 1's promise gets audited in the cloud era: the vault may be ciphertext, but the key material, the sharing keys and the settings that decide how hard the master password is to guess all still reach a server that can rewrite them. The verdict is not that the promise is false, but that it stays unverified until the protocol is argued against a malicious server. Chapter 13 stops asking a server to keep a shared secret at all: it hands over a public key, and the secret never leaves the device.

## Open / unverified items

- **"25 vs 27"**: the handoff flagged secondary reports of 25 or 27 total attacks. The USENIX three-vendor PDF states **12 + 7 + 6 = 25**; the ePrint *four-vendor* full version states 12 + 7 + 6 + 6 (1Password) = 31. Neither source yields 27, so "27" appears to be inaccurate and is not used. The chapter uses **25**.
- **Author order**: ePrint metadata lists only Torrisi, Backendal and Paterson, and ETH press coverage names Scarlata. The PDF byline (Scarlata first) is treated as authoritative per the task and WRITING-SPEC; this discrepancy is noted in the byline note.
- **Short-form attacks deferred to the full version**: BW03 (Key Connector), BW04, BW10, LP05, LP06 and the DL03/DL06 mechanism details are described only briefly in the published version ("deferred to the full version [48]"). Their Table 1 names, causes and impacts are reported, but their step-by-step mechanics are not asserted here.
- **Table 1 interaction counts** (`104`/`105 syncs`) are rendered without visible superscripts in the extracted text; interpreted as $10^{4}$/$10^{5}$ from context, and stated as an order-of-magnitude range rather than an exact figure.
- **Market-share 23%** and the "majority recover passwords" phrasing are the paper's own claims (the 23% is derived by the authors, and the figure is disputed by Dashlane per footnote 1); reported as the paper states, not re-derived.
- **Dashlane architecture** is acknowledged by the authors as partly reverse-engineered and possibly incomplete (only the CLI is fully open source, and it lacks encryption/sharing).
